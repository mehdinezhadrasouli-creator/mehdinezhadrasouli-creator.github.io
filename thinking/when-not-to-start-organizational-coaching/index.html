Coaching in Organizations

When Is It Better Not to Start Organizational Coaching?

Sometimes the issue is not coaching quality; it is whether the conditions are ready

14 September 202616 min readPractice essay

Core question

When should an organization delay the start of coaching because the conditions are not yet ready?

In brief

Sometimes the issue is not coaching quality; it is whether the conditions are ready

Evidence note

This article draws on my practical experience with managerial issues and organizational coaching programs, interpreted alongside professional standards and independent research. The readiness questions are a practical diagnostic lens, not a validated predictive model.

01

Sometimes the most professional response is “Not yet”

Sometimes the most professional response to a request for coaching is:

“Not yet.”

Not because coaching is the wrong intervention.

But because some situations have not reached the point where coaching can work on them effectively.

A manager may be unhappy with someone’s performance but still has not given that person clear feedback.

An organization may say a leader needs to “work on leadership” but cannot explain what should actually look different in the person’s work.

Or we may expect a manager to make more independent decisions while the real decision authority still sits somewhere else.

In situations like these, starting coaching quickly may not solve the problem.

It may even make the problem less clear.

02

When coaching replaces a management conversation

Imagine a manager is dissatisfied with an employee’s performance.

They have discussed the person with HR for some time.

They have examples.

But the employee still does not know exactly what the manager is dissatisfied with.

Then someone suggests:

“Let’s get them a coach.”

My first question in that situation is:

Is there a conversation the manager needs to have themselves?

Coaching cannot replace clear feedback, expectation-setting or performance management.

If someone needs to know what is not working in their performance, the organization needs to say it directly.

A coach can later help the person think through that feedback, see their own contribution, make choices and build new behaviour.

But the coach should not become the carrier of a message the manager is unwilling to deliver.

That boundary matters.

If coaching feels to the coachee like a hidden corrective process, trust can be damaged before the work has properly started.

Research on organizational coaching context also suggests that how an organization introduces a program before it begins matters. In a scenario-based experimental study with 175 participants, developmental and remedial framings of coaching did not have the same relationship with pre-coaching motivation. The study examined motivation before coaching; it did not establish downstream coaching effectiveness.

03

When the person does not know why they were selected for coaching

Imagine an organization tells a manager:

“We have arranged a coach for you.”

But the manager does not know why.

Are they being prepared for a bigger role?

Is there a performance concern?

Is there a specific capability they are expected to develop?

Or did their manager simply think coaching would “be good for them”?

At that point, the problem may not yet be coaching.

The problem may be clarity.

In a study of unsuccessful workplace coaching experiences, 13 predominantly negative experiences were identified among 357 coachees. The analysis highlighted three broad themes: mismatched expectations, problems in the coach–coachee relationship and problematic organizational involvement, including hidden agendas.

So before coaching starts, we should at least be able to answer one question:

Why does this program exist, and what is it intended to help with?

I explored this boundary in more detail in Organizational coaching starts with the contract, not the first session.

04

When the organization has a goal, but the coachee does not yet own it

The organization may have a reasonable expectation.

Perhaps the manager needs to delegate more.

Collaborate more effectively with peers.

Act more clearly in difficult decisions.

Or prepare for a larger role.

But the fact that the organization has an expectation does not mean we can simply turn it into a coaching goal without a conversation.

The organization can clarify its expectation; it cannot decide exactly what the coachee must understand or change inside the coaching sessions.

The 2025 ICF Core Competencies emphasize clear agreements around goals, responsibilities and the client’s commitment to work on their goals. The overall purpose of the engagement and the desired outcome of each session are developed in partnership with the client.

If someone has entered the program only to comply with an organizational requirement and still sees no issue they want to work on, another conversation may need to happen before coaching begins.

That does not necessarily mean abandoning coaching.

It may simply mean that we are not ready to start yet.

05

When we cannot say what is supposed to change

“We want them to become a better leader.”

“They need more confidence.”

“They need to be more effective in their management role.”

“Their communication needs to improve.”

These can all be useful starting points for a conversation.

But they are not yet enough for an organizational coaching program.

If nobody can explain what should look observably different after several months, it will also be difficult later to judge whether coaching had an effect.

In How Do We Know Organizational Coaching Actually Worked? I argued that a goal becomes more useful for measurement when it can be connected to observable behaviour or work outcomes.

Instead of “improve leadership,” we might ask:

Do we expect fewer decisions to be escalated to the direct manager?

Do team members receive more decision authority?

Do difficult conversations happen earlier?

Can the manager take a clearer position when there is disagreement?

If we still do not agree on the expected change, we may be rushing the start of the engagement.

06

When we ask for change but do not make change possible

We may ask a manager to delegate more, make more independent decisions, take more risks or give the team more responsibility.

At the same time, the organization may have tightly restricted decision authority.

The direct manager may continue to intervene in decisions.

Or the cost of making a mistake may be so high that there is effectively no room to experiment with new behaviour.

In that environment, coaching may help the person see their own contribution more clearly.

But it cannot remove a structural constraint.

So before starting, we need to ask:

Does this person actually have the opportunity to behave in the way we say we want?

I examined the individual-versus-system diagnosis in Before Coaching the Leader, Check the System.

The question here comes one step later:

Once we have identified the constraint, have we acted on it enough for coaching to make sense?

Sometimes the answer is no.

And in that case, the system may need attention first.

07

When the organization wants results but will not support the change

Assume the goal is clear.

The person is also willing to work on it.

But after every coaching session they return to exactly the same environment.

The direct manager provides no feedback, creates no opportunity to practise and the work context gives the person little room to test a different behaviour.

Then the entire burden of change is placed on the coachee and the coach.

That is not a realistic expectation.

Research on negative coaching experiences has also identified organizational support as part of the context that can contribute to how coaching is experienced, alongside the risks of inappropriate organizational interference.

I discussed this boundary in more detail in What Role Should the Direct Manager Play in Organizational Coaching?.

The manager should not enter the confidential content of the sessions.

But if a new behaviour is expected to survive in the workplace, the workplace also needs to make room for it.

08

When coaching is expected to become a hidden reporting system

Another serious warning sign appears when the organization expects the coach to pass on the content of sessions.

For example:

“Tell us what the real problem is.”

“After each session, tell us what you worked on.”

“Tell us whether you think this person is suitable for the role.”

At this point the issue is no longer only effectiveness.

It is trust and professional ethics.

The ICF Code of Ethics requires clarity before coaching begins about roles, responsibilities and confidentiality among client, sponsor and coach. It also requires clear agreement about what information will be exchanged among the parties and how that information will be shared.

If the organization needs an assessment, it should design an assessment.

If it needs a performance decision, that belongs in the performance-management process.

Coaching should not become another name for hidden assessment.

09

“Not yet” is different from “never”

Most of the situations described here do not mean coaching is a bad choice.

Usually they mean something needs to happen first.

Feedback needs to be given.

The goal needs to become clearer.

The coachee needs to have a real voice in defining the goal.

Decision authority may need to change.

Confidentiality boundaries need to be agreed.

Or the direct manager needs to take responsibility for their part of the process.

After that, coaching may be exactly the intervention that is needed.

That distinction matters to me.

If coaching starts too early, we may later blame the coach, the coachee or coaching itself, when the real problem was that the conditions were not ready for the work to begin.

010

What do I look for before starting?

I do not treat these as a validated assessment tool or a checklist that predicts success. They are simply practical questions that help me think more carefully before an engagement starts:

Is the issue clear enough to both the coachee and the organization?

Is the person willing to work on it and do they have a real voice in defining the goal?

Can the expected change be observed in behaviour or real work?

Does the person have at least enough authority, support and room to practise the new behaviour?

Have we agreed on the manager’s role, confidentiality and what information can move between the parties?

If several important answers are still “no,” it may be better not to rush the start of the program.

The conditions need attention first.

011

Perhaps the first question is not “Who should be coached?”

Organizations often ask:

“Who should we select for coaching?”

But there may be a question that comes before that:

“Is this issue, at this time and under these conditions, actually ready for coaching?”

Sometimes the answer is yes.

Sometimes another intervention needs to happen first.

And sometimes only a few things need to be clarified before the work begins.

I see that diagnosis as part of the quality of organizational coaching.

The goal is not to recommend coaching for every problem.

It is to know when coaching can help, and when it is better not to start yet.

012

About the evidence behind this article

This article draws on my practical experience with managerial issues and organizational coaching programs, interpreted alongside professional standards and independent research.

A recurring theme in my professional experience has been that before recommending or designing an intervention, the problem, decision authority, ownership of implementation and review criteria need to be sufficiently clear. I treat this as a professional diagnostic principle, not as a validated scientific model.

The available research also does not justify claiming that a particular readiness checklist can predict coaching success or failure. A more defensible conclusion is that the context in which coaching begins matters: how the program is introduced, clarity of expectations, quality of agreement, client ownership and organizational behaviour around the engagement can shape the coaching experience.

So the claim of this article is deliberately limited:

Before organizational coaching begins, greater clarity about goals, roles, coachee ownership, organizational context and confidentiality boundaries can reduce the risk of starting an ambiguous or poorly matched engagement.

Why We Delay Difficult Conversations

When the real issue is not only what to say

11 September 202614 min readPractice essay

Core question

What has kept this difficult conversation from happening so far?

In brief

When the real issue is not only what to say

Evidence note

Drawn from recurring patterns in executive coaching sessions with managers, described without client stories, quotations, outcomes or identifying detail. ICF Core Competencies and PCC assessment criteria inform the coaching stance. Harvard Business Review essays on executive conflict avoidance and when to skip a difficult conversation are cited as external context, not as proof of any client result. Clarity, respect and accountability are a practice framing from work with managers, not a validated research model.

01

The question is not always only “What should I say?”

In several coaching sessions with managers, the conversation has started in almost the same place:

“How do I give this feedback?”

It is a completely reasonable question.

A manager may be looking for a better sentence, unsure where to begin, or wanting to be sure the feedback lands in a usable way.

What has interested me in some of these conversations is that, after a pause and a little more exploration, the issue has not always been only “What should I say?”

Sometimes the manager already largely knew what needed to be said.

They recognised the behaviour that needed to change, their expectation was fairly clear, and they may even have rehearsed the conversation in their mind more than once.

And still, the conversation did not happen.

That is where another question becomes important for me:

What has kept this conversation from happening until now?

02

A difficult conversation is not always a skills problem

When a conversation feels hard, it is natural to reach for technique.

How should I open?

Which words should I use?

How do I give feedback without making the other person defensive?

Those questions matter. They are not always the first question.

Sometimes, before we turn to a model or a technique, we need to understand a little more about what has created the delay.

In coaching, the question may be:

“When you think about having this conversation, what becomes difficult for you?”

Or:

“What has kept you from having this conversation until now?”

The client may talk about concern for the relationship.

They may talk about the other person’s likely reaction.

They may say they are still not sure they are right.

Or something entirely different may surface.

The point, for me, is that the coach should not decide in advance why the delay exists.

If we ask from the outset, “What are you afraid of?” when the client has not named fear at all, we are importing an interpretation into the conversation.

But if the client themselves says, “I think I’m afraid the relationship will be damaged,” then we can ask:

“When you talk about fear of damaging the relationship, what comes up in your mind?”

Here the question grows from language the client has already brought in, not from the coach’s assumption.

That small difference is, for me, one of the important differences between coaching and diagnosing the issue from the outside.

03

Sometimes we invent two false choices for ourselves

In some of the conversations I have held, the manager saw themselves between two options:

Either speak bluntly and damage the relationship.

Or say nothing for now, to protect the relationship.

But are those really the only two options?

That question matters to me, because sometimes the binary itself is part of what has stopped the manager.

The real choice may not be between candour and relationship.

Perhaps the question is:

How can I raise what needs to be said clearly, while still treating the other person with respect?

That is the point where a difficult conversation moves beyond a simple communication technique and becomes part of leadership responsibility.

This is not only my experience. Harvard Business Review has also written that even experienced executives may postpone or avoid emotionally charged situations.

04

Delay can have consequences too

One thing that has stood out in sessions is that delay does not always make the issue easier.

Sometimes a manager postpones a conversation because they do not want the other person to feel hurt.

In that interval, they themselves become more distressed.

The behaviour continues.

Judgements accumulate.

And when the conversation finally happens, it is no longer only about one specific behaviour.

Weeks or months of feeling and interpretation have entered the room as well.

In one case, that accumulated pressure meant that when the manager finally raised the issue, the tone was much sharper than they had intended.

By contrast, I have also seen direct and respectful conversations reduce some of the tension and judgement that had already formed, rather than damage the relationship.

Of course, a few experiences do not prove that every difficult conversation will improve a relationship.

The larger point is that postponing the conversation is not a choice without consequences either.

Conflict-management literature points to a similar tension. Avoidance may feel easier in the short term, and in some situations it can intensify both the issue and the feelings around it.

05

Before finding the sentence, notice what has delayed the conversation

When a manager asks, “How do I give this feedback?”, it is tempting to start crafting the sentence at once.

But a few questions may be worth exploring first:

“What has kept this conversation from happening until now?”

“When you think about this conversation, what matters to you?”

“In this situation, what has become hard for you?”

These questions are not meant to steer the client toward a particular answer.

The theme may be the relationship.

It may be power.

The manager may doubt their own judgement.

Or they may realise they still do not have enough information for the conversation.

The coach’s job here is not to find the cause on the client’s behalf.

The coach’s job is to create space in which the client can see the issue more clearly and make their own meaning of it.

That logic aligns with the ICF approach, which asks the coach to listen to the client’s language, experience, feelings and context, and to use open inquiry to facilitate the client’s own awareness, rather than substituting the coach’s answer or diagnosis.

06

Three things that matter to me in a difficult conversation

When the issue is clearer and the time for the conversation has arrived, I usually think about three things:

Clarity, respect and accountability.

07

Clarity

Can the manager say what they have observed without staying at the level of generalities?

Which behaviour or result has become problematic?

What is their expectation?

And what needs to change?

Sentences such as “You need to be more professional” or “Fix your attitude” may feel clear to the manager, but they do not necessarily tell the other person which behaviour is meant to change.

08

Respect

Being clear is not the same as being harsh.

It is possible to talk about performance or a specific behaviour without issuing a verdict on the person’s character.

There is a large difference between these two sentences:

“In the last three meetings, the report was delivered after the deadline.”

And:

“You are an irresponsible person.”

The first is about something that can be discussed.

The second targets the person’s identity.

09

Accountability

The conversation does not end when the sentence has been said.

What if the other person sees it differently?

What if they become upset?

What if they agree, but the behaviour does not change?

What responsibility does the manager hold for what comes next?

Sometimes the main difficulty is not starting the conversation, but staying in it.

That means being able to hear the other person’s reaction without immediately withdrawing the message or turning the exchange into a fight.

These three elements are not a proven research model for me. They are more a framing that has formed from working with managers when thinking about conversations of this kind.

010

Does every issue need a difficult conversation?

No.

That is an important part of the story as well.

Not every irritation, disagreement or small behaviour needs to be turned at once into a formal conversation.

Sometimes we do not yet have enough information.

Sometimes our interpretation needs checking.

Sometimes emotion is high enough that a little distance helps.

And sometimes the issue is not important enough to reopen.

Even sources on difficult conversations recommend checking first whether the conversation is truly needed and what it is meant to resolve.

That is different from knowing an important issue exists and simply moving it to next week again and again.

If the behaviour keeps recurring, performance is being harmed, the relationship is wearing down, or we ourselves are becoming more entangled in the issue every day, delay may no longer be only “waiting for the right time.”

The delay itself may have become part of the problem.

011

Three questions before the next conversation

If there is a conversation you have been postponing for a while, these three questions may be worth considering before you search for the perfect sentence:

1. What has kept this conversation from happening until now?

2. What needs to become clear in this conversation?

3. If nothing changes after the conversation, what is my responsibility as a manager?

The answers may help clarify what conversation is actually needed.

And sometimes they may reveal that the issue is different from what we first imagined.

For me, that is where coaching can create value.

Not by writing a sentence for the manager to memorise and deliver in the meeting.

But by helping them see the situation more precisely, make a more conscious choice, and own the responsibility that comes with that choice.

A difficult conversation may still remain difficult.

The aim of coaching is not necessarily to make it easy.

Sometimes the aim is simply for the manager to see more clearly:

What needs to be said, why saying it has become hard, and what kind of manager they want to be in this situation.

When Your Manager Is the Problem: What Part Is Still Yours to Influence?

Seeing your part is not the same as blaming yourself

12 September 202616 min readPractice essay

Core question

In this situation, what part is still mine to influence?

In brief

Seeing your part is not the same as blaming yourself

Evidence note

Drawn from an initial analysis of 48 coaching clients and more than 100 coaching sessions, described without client stories, quotations, outcomes or identifying detail. “Manager as a Mirror” is a recurring observation from this coaching practice, not a scientific model or psychological diagnosis. Leader–Member Exchange meta-analyses and Harvard Business Review’s Managing Your Boss are cited as external context on relationship quality and clarifying mutual expectations. ICF Core Competencies and PCC assessment criteria inform the coaching stance. Formal power between manager and employee is not assumed to be equal.

01

Seeing your part is not the same as blaming yourself

“My manager is the problem.”

I hear versions of this sentence often in executive coaching sessions.

A manager who does not give feedback. A manager who controls too much. A manager who does not seem to notice the work. A manager who is unclear in decisions. Or a manager with whom conversation has become so difficult that distance feels easier.

Sometimes the manager really is the problem.

Coaching should not reduce every harmful behavior to “the client’s perception.” Nor should it ask someone in an unequal power relationship to take responsibility for their manager’s behavior.

But there is another question as well:

In this situation, what part is still mine to influence?

For me, this is often where a conversation can move from focusing only on the other person’s behavior toward seeing the choices that still belong to the individual.

02

The relationship with a manager has been one of the recurring themes in my sessions

In an initial analysis of 48 clients and more than 100 coaching sessions, the relationship with a manager was one of the recurring patterns.

The issues differed: a demanding manager, a controlling manager, feeling overlooked or treated unfairly, lack of feedback, conflict, difficulty setting boundaries, or not feeling seen.

But in some of these conversations, as the issue opened further, the question was no longer only:

“What is wrong with my manager?”

Other questions also entered the conversation:

Have I clearly said what I want?

Am I holding an expectation we have never actually discussed?

What do I do when I disagree?

When I do not feel heard, how do I respond?

Where have I set a boundary, and where have I only stayed dissatisfied?

And sometimes a more important question takes shape:

“What pattern am I repeating in this relationship?”

This has become one of the notable patterns in my coaching sessions. In my own internal notes, I have called it “Manager as a Mirror.”

It is not a scientific model or a psychological diagnosis. It is simply a recurring observation from my coaching experience.

03

“My part” is not the same as “my fault”

These two are easy to confuse.

Suppose a manager constantly changes decisions, withholds necessary information, or treats team members inconsistently.

A meaningful part of the problem may genuinely sit with the manager’s behavior.

Seeing your own part does not mean saying:

“So this was my fault.”

A different question arises:

“Given these conditions, what choices do I still have that I have not tried?”

That distinction matters.

Research on leader–employee relationships has shown that the quality of that relationship is linked with variables such as trust, job satisfaction and performance. The relationship is not created by only one side, even though the manager’s formal position and power give the manager an important role in shaping it.

So a two-sided relationship should not be mistaken for equal power.

A manager usually holds more formal power.

That does not mean the individual has no agency in how they meet the situation.

04

Sometimes we expect a manager to know something we have never said

This is one pattern I have found particularly interesting in sessions.

A client may say:

“They do not see me at all.”

“They have no idea how much I am doing.”

“They never give me an opportunity.”

“They do not know I am ready for the next role.”

Any of that may be accurate.

Then a simple question may come up:

“What have you actually shared with them about this so far?”

And sometimes the answer is something like:

“Not very directly.”

That is where the framing of the problem shifts a little.

We are no longer dealing only with a manager who does not know something. We are dealing with a relationship in which some important information has never been exchanged at all.

In the management literature, this is often discussed as managing the relationship with your boss — “managing up.”

That does not mean flattery or accommodating every managerial behavior. It is about clarifying expectations, priorities and ways of communicating, and building a workable relationship between two people who depend on each other to get the work done.

05

Sometimes the issue is not whether we speak, but how we speak

Sometimes the client has already spoken with their manager many times.

In those situations, it is not useful to say: “Well, just go and talk.”

The issue may be the shape of the conversation.

Someone may stay silent for a long time, let frustration build, and then raise everything at once in a single meeting.

Or they may express the request so indirectly that the other person never understands how important the issue is.

Or before the conversation begins, the outcome already feels decided:

“They will not agree.”

“They will get defensive again.”

“They never listen.”

In coaching, the coach should not decide for the client whether that reading is right or wrong.

One can ask:

“When you say talking to them does not work, what experience has led you to that conclusion?”

Or:

“What happened in the previous conversations?”

And then:

“What would you want to be different this time?”

These questions do not begin with the assumption that the person is mistaken. They only help them examine their experience more carefully.

That boundary matters to me.

In coaching, the coach’s analysis should not replace the client’s experience. The coach’s role is more about helping the client see, clarify and discover choices that may have been hard to notice inside the noise of the problem.

When the harder question is what has kept a needed conversation from happening at all, the companion essay on why we delay difficult conversations takes that delay further.

06

Three areas worth examining

When the issue is the relationship with a manager, I do not look for one fixed formula.

But three areas can be useful to think with.

07

Clarity

Does my manager actually know what I want from them?

Have we discussed priorities, role, decision rights, feedback or growth path clearly enough?

Or is an important part of my expectations still only in my own head?

08

Boundaries

Where have I agreed to something that was not realistically achievable?

Where have I disagreed, but never said so?

Where have I allowed an issue to drag on because I was worried about the relationship?

A boundary is not necessarily confrontation.

Sometimes it can be as simple as:

“To deliver this by Monday, one of the other two priorities will need to move. Which one matters more to you?”

09

The assumptions we have built about the other person

After several difficult experiences, our prediction of a manager’s behavior can harden into a settled fact in our minds.

“They never listen.”

“They do not trust me.”

“They do not want me to grow.”

Those readings may be accurate.

But another question is still worth examining:

“Which part do you know, and which part have you interpreted?”

The aim is not to undermine the person’s experience.

The aim is to distinguish what actually happened from the meaning we have attached to it.

Sometimes that distinction brings other options into view.

010

Not every relationship is fixed by managing it better

This part matters to me.

Sometimes a manager’s behavior is genuinely harmful.

Sometimes the organization does not allow healthy dialogue.

Sometimes the issue is not only the relationship between two people. Power structures, organizational culture, unfairness or a lack of psychological safety can also be part of the problem.

In those situations, “look at your part” must not become advice to endure what should not be endured.

Seeing your part is different from accepting every behavior.

Sometimes the right choice is to repair the relationship.

Sometimes the issue needs to be escalated to a higher level in the organization.

Sometimes it is necessary to seek help from human resources or someone else in the organization.

And sometimes a person may conclude that they no longer want to stay in that relationship — or even in that organization.

Coaching is not there to persuade someone to stay.

It is there to help them see the situation more clearly and make their own choice more consciously.

011

Four questions before concluding about your manager

If the relationship with a manager has become one of the main sources of pressure at work, these four questions can be a useful starting point.

1. What specific behavior or event is creating the problem for me?

Instead of “my manager is a difficult person,” what concrete event has happened?

2. What has still not been discussed clearly between us?

The answer may be “nothing.” That answer also gives us important information.

3. In this relationship, what is within my influence, and what is not?

That distinction often clarifies part of the issue.

4. In meeting this situation, what kind of professional or manager do I want to be?

For me, this question is more useful than “How do I change my manager?”

The manager may change, or they may not.

But the person can still decide how they show up in the situation.

012

Seeing your part does not let the manager off the hook

That may be the most important point.

Seeing your part in a difficult relationship does not mean overlooking the other person’s behavior.

It means finding, among all the things you cannot control, the part where you can still choose:

A clearer request.

A firmer boundary.

A conversation that has not happened.

An assumption that needs examining.

A behavior you have repeated.

Or even a decision to leave a relationship that is no longer repairable.

In my experience, growth in our relationship with power begins where a person neither places everything on the manager nor places everything on themselves.

They become able to hold both at once:

What is genuinely the problem outside them, and what remains open to choice in how they respond.

Sometimes seeing that gap between “what I cannot change” and “what I can still choose” is an important part of what happens in coaching.

Not so that someone hands us a ready-made answer.

So that we can see the issue with more clarity and make our own decision.

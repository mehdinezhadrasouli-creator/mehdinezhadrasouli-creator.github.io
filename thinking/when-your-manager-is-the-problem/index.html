When Your Manager Is the Problem, What Can You Still Control?

Seeing your part is not the same as blaming yourself

12 September 202616 min readPractice essay

Core question

In this situation, what is still within my control?

In brief

Seeing your part is not the same as blaming yourself

Evidence note

Drawn from an initial analysis of 48 coaching clients and more than 100 coaching sessions, described without client stories, quotations, outcomes or identifying detail. “Manager as a Mirror” is a recurring observation from this coaching practice, not a scientific model or psychological diagnosis. Leader–Member Exchange meta-analyses and Harvard Business Review’s Managing Your Boss are cited as external context on relationship quality and clarifying mutual expectations. ICF Core Competencies and PCC assessment criteria inform the coaching stance. Formal power between manager and employee is not assumed to be equal.

01

Sometimes the manager really is part of the problem

“My manager is the problem.”

I have heard versions of this sentence many times in coaching conversations.

A manager who does not give feedback.

A manager who controls too much.

A manager who does not seem to notice the work.

A manager who is inconsistent or unclear in decisions.

Or a manager with whom conversation has become so difficult that distance feels easier.

Sometimes the manager really is a significant part of the problem.

Coaching should not reduce every harmful behavior to “the client’s perception,” and it should not ask someone in an unequal power relationship to take responsibility for their manager’s behavior.

But there is another question worth asking:

In this situation, what is still within my control?

For me, this is often where a coaching conversation can move from focusing only on the external problem toward seeing the choices that still belong to the person.

02

The manager relationship is one of the recurring themes I see in coaching

In an initial analysis of 48 clients and more than 100 coaching sessions, the relationship with a manager was one of the recurring themes.

The situations were different: a demanding manager, a controlling manager, feeling overlooked, unfairness, lack of feedback, conflict, weak boundaries, or not feeling seen.

But as some of these conversations opened up, the question was no longer only:

“What is wrong with my manager?”

Other questions began to appear:

Have I clearly said what I need?

Am I holding an expectation we have never actually discussed?

What do I do when I disagree?

When I do not feel heard, do I withdraw, push harder, or try a different approach?

Where have I set a boundary, and where have I only stayed dissatisfied?

And perhaps most importantly:

What pattern am I repeating in this relationship?

This has become a useful recurring observation in my coaching work — something I think of as Manager as a Mirror.

It is not a scientific model or a psychological diagnosis. It is a pattern I have repeatedly noticed in real coaching conversations.

03

“My part” is not the same as “my fault”

These two ideas are easy to confuse.

Imagine a manager who constantly changes decisions, withholds necessary information, or treats team members inconsistently.

A meaningful part of the problem may genuinely belong to the manager.

Seeing your own part does not mean saying:

“So this was my fault.”

A more useful question is:

Given these conditions, what choices do I still have that I have not tried?

That distinction matters.

Research on the quality of leader–employee relationships has linked that relationship with outcomes including performance, trust and job satisfaction. It also suggests that the relationship is not created by only one side, even though the leader’s formal position and power give the leader a particularly important role.

A relationship can be two-sided without the power being equal.

A manager holds more formal power.

That does not mean the employee has no agency in how they respond to the situation.

04

Sometimes we expect a manager to know something we have never said

This is one pattern I have found particularly interesting in coaching.

A client may say:

“They do not see me.”

“They have no idea how much I am doing.”

“They never give me an opportunity.”

“They do not know I am ready for the next role.”

Any of those statements may be accurate.

Then a simple question comes up:

What have you actually shared with your manager about this so far?

Sometimes the answer is something like:

“Not very directly.”

At that point, the problem changes slightly.

It is no longer only about a manager who does not know. It is also about a working relationship in which some important information has never been exchanged.

The literature on managing the relationship with your manager has long emphasized the value of clarifying expectations, priorities, communication preferences and mutual needs.

This is not about flattering a manager or tolerating any behavior. It is about building a workable relationship when two people depend on each other to get the work done.

05

Sometimes the issue is not whether we speak, but how we speak

In some cases, the client has already spoken with the manager several times.

So “just go and talk to them” is not useful advice.

The issue may be how those conversations happen.

Someone may stay silent for a long time, allow frustration to build, and then raise everything at once.

Or the request may be so indirect that the manager never understands how important the issue is.

Or the outcome may already feel decided before the conversation begins:

“They will never agree.”

“They will get defensive again.”

“They never listen.”

In coaching, I do not want to decide for the client whether that interpretation is right or wrong.

A question might be:

When you say talking to them does not work, what experiences have led you to that conclusion?

Or:

What happened in the previous conversations?

And then:

What would you want to be different this time?

Those questions do not begin with the assumption that the client is mistaken. They help the client look more closely at their own experience.

That boundary matters to me because the coach’s interpretation should not replace the client’s experience.

When the harder question is what has kept a needed conversation from happening at all, the companion essay on why we delay difficult conversations takes that delay further.

06

Three areas worth examining

When the issue is a difficult relationship with a manager, I do not look for one fixed formula.

But three areas are often useful to examine.

07

Clarity

Does my manager actually know what I need from them?

Have we discussed priorities, role expectations, decision rights, feedback or growth clearly enough?

Or are many of my expectations still only in my own head?

08

Boundaries

Where have I agreed to something that was not realistically achievable?

Where have I disagreed but never said so?

Where have I allowed an issue to continue because I was worried about damaging the relationship?

A boundary does not automatically mean confrontation.

Sometimes it sounds as simple as:

“To deliver this by Monday, one of the other two priorities will need to move. Which one matters more?”

09

The assumptions we have built about the other person

After several difficult experiences, our prediction about a manager can start to feel like a confirmed fact:

“They never listen.”

“They do not trust me.”

“They do not want me to grow.”

Those interpretations may be accurate.

But another question can still be useful:

What part do you know, and what part are you interpreting?

The purpose is not to invalidate someone’s experience. It is to create a little distance between observable events and the meaning we have attached to them.

Sometimes that distance reveals choices that were difficult to see before.

010

Not every manager relationship can be fixed by managing it better

This matters.

Sometimes a manager is genuinely harmful.

Sometimes the organization does not support healthy dialogue.

Sometimes the issue is not only the relationship. Power structures, culture, discrimination, unfairness or lack of psychological safety may also be involved.

In those situations, “look at your part” must not become advice to tolerate the intolerable.

Sometimes the more mature choice is to repair the relationship.

Sometimes the issue needs to be raised with another person or at another level in the organization.

Sometimes organizational support is necessary.

And sometimes the conclusion may be that you no longer want to stay in that relationship — or even in that organization.

Coaching is not there to persuade someone to stay.

It is there to help them see their choices more clearly and take ownership of the choice they make.

011

Four questions worth asking before you reach a conclusion about your manager

If the relationship with your manager has become one of the main sources of pressure at work, these four questions can be a useful starting point:

1. What specific behavior or event is creating the problem for me?

Not “my manager is difficult,” but what has actually happened?

2. What has still not been discussed clearly between us?

The answer may be “nothing.” That is useful information too.

3. What is within my control in this relationship, and what is not?

That distinction often creates important clarity.

4. Who do I want to be professionally when I respond to this situation?

For me, this question is more useful than “How do I change my manager?”

The manager may change, or they may not.

How I choose to show up in the situation is something I can still decide.

012

Seeing your part does not let the manager off the hook

That may be the most important point.

Seeing your own part in a difficult relationship does not mean ignoring the other person’s behavior.

It means finding, among all the things you cannot control, the part where you still have a choice:

A clearer request.

A firmer boundary.

A conversation that has not happened.

An assumption that needs to be tested.

A behavior you have repeated yourself.

Or even a decision to leave a relationship that is no longer repairable.

In my experience, growth in our relationship with power begins when we neither place everything on the manager nor place everything on ourselves.

We become able to hold both at the same time:

What is genuinely wrong outside us, and what remains ours to choose in how we respond.

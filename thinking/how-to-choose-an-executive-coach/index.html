How to Choose the Right Executive Coach

Seven things worth checking before you start working together

11 September 2026Updated 24 September 20265 min readPractice essay

Core question

What should you check before choosing an executive coach?

In brief

Choose an executive coach by checking recognized credentials, fit, confidentiality, professional boundaries and whether the conversation helps you think more clearly while keeping ownership of your decisions.

Evidence note

Drawn from professional coaching practice and publicly available ICF guidance on credentials, coach selection and ethics. Session patterns are described without client stories, quotations, outcomes or identifying detail. This is practical guidance for choosing a coach, not a claim that any single credential or conversation guarantees fit.

01

A significant part of the work depends on the relationship

When people start looking for an executive coach, they usually check the obvious things first: background, qualifications, years of experience and area of specialization.

All of those matter. I do not think they are enough.

After more than 700 hours of coaching, one thing has become increasingly clear to me: the quality of coaching does not depend only on how well a coach asks questions or how many models and tools they know.

A significant part of the work depends on the relationship that forms between coach and client.

Can you actually think with this person?

Can you talk about something that may not yet be fully clear even to you?

When you get stuck, will this person hand you an answer, or help you see more clearly what is happening?

If I had to suggest a practical way to choose an executive coach, these are the seven things I would pay most attention to.

02

The coach should be able to see the real work context

Executive coaching does not happen in a vacuum.

A leader works inside a real system. There is a CEO, a team, targets, organizational politics, limited resources and sometimes decisions with no clean answer.

One pattern I have seen repeatedly is that a question that first appears personal is not always only personal.

A client may say, 'I have lost confidence.' But as the conversation develops, it becomes clear that their role is ambiguous and two senior stakeholders are asking for contradictory things.

Or they may say, 'My team is underperforming.' Later it becomes clear that a conversation that needed to happen three months ago still has not happened.

A good coach should be able to see the person without losing sight of the system around them.

There is an important boundary, though.

Understanding the business context does not mean automatically becoming a consultant.

If a coach moves into another role because of their background or expertise, such as advisor, HR specialist or consultant, that shift should be made explicit.

Coaching and advisory work can both be useful.

The important thing is to know which work you are doing at any given moment.

03

So how do you choose the right executive coach?

I would not look for 'the best coach.'

I would look for the coach who is right for you and for the question you are facing now.

Check their recognized coaching credentials.

Look at their experience.

Ask about confidentiality.

Clarify the boundary between coaching and advisory work.

Most importantly, notice what happens to your thinking when you talk with them.

If you leave the conversation only impressed by the coach, that is not enough.

But if you can see your own question more clearly, notice something you could not see before, and still feel that the decision belongs to you, that is probably a better sign.

For me, the simplest summary is this:

Choose someone who helps you think better, not someone who thinks for you.

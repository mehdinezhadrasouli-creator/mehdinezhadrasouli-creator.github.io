How to Choose the Right Executive Coach

Seven things worth checking before you start working together

11 September 202612 min readPractice essay

Core question

What should you check before choosing an executive coach?

In brief

Seven things worth checking before you start working together

Evidence note

Drawn from professional coaching practice and publicly available ICF guidance on credentials, coach selection and ethics. Session patterns are described without client stories, quotations, outcomes or identifying detail. This is practical guidance for choosing a coach, not a claim that any single credential or conversation guarantees fit.

01

A significant part of the work depends on the relationship

When people start looking for an executive coach, they usually check the obvious things first: background, qualifications, years of experience and area of specialization.

All of those matter. I do not think they are enough.

After more than 700 hours of coaching, one thing has become increasingly clear to me: the quality of coaching does not depend only on how well a coach asks questions or how many models and tools they know.

A significant part of the work depends on the relationship that forms between coach and client.

Can you actually think with this person?

Can you talk about something that may not yet be fully clear even to you?

When you get stuck, will this person hand you an answer, or help you see more clearly what is happening?

If I had to suggest a practical way to choose an executive coach, these are the seven things I would pay most attention to.

02

Do not stop at the title 'Executive Coach'

A lot of people use the title 'coach.' The title alone tells you very little about someone's training, experience or professional standards.

One useful thing to check is a recognized coaching credential, especially one issued by a well-established professional body such as the International Coaching Federation (ICF).

A credential does not guarantee that a coach is the right fit for you. But it does indicate that the person has gone through a defined path of coach education, experience and assessment, and is accountable to professional and ethical standards.

For me, a recognized coaching credential is best treated as an initial filter, not as the final reason to choose someone.

Then the more important question begins:

Is this coach right for me?

03

Do not underestimate the discovery conversation

I do not see a discovery conversation as a sales meeting.

Its first purpose is to help both sides decide whether working together makes sense.

A coach may be highly experienced, hold strong credentials and have worked with many leaders, and still not be the right coach for you.

Pay attention to what happens in that first conversation.

Do you feel able to speak openly?

Does the coach genuinely listen, or mainly wait for a chance to talk about themselves?

Do they take time to understand your question, or do they diagnose the problem too quickly?

Coaching is difficult without trust.

At some point you may want to talk about uncertainty, fear, conflict, feeling inadequate, a difficult relationship with your manager, a consequential decision, or something you would not discuss freely with most people at work.

If you already feel that you need to perform, censor yourself or hide parts of the situation in the first conversation, that is useful information.

04

Notice who still owns the decisions

This is one of the most important criteria for me.

A coach is not there to make your decisions for you.

If the relationship reaches a point where, for every important decision, you are waiting for the coach to say, 'If I were you, I would do this,' it is worth reconsidering what kind of relationship has developed.

In many of the sessions I have held, the client already had some part of the answer. The difficulty was that it had become buried under workload, pressure, other people's expectations, anxiety or mental noise.

The coach's role is not to replace the client's answer with their own.

The work is to help the question become clearer, bring assumptions into view, open other angles and help the client reach a decision they can genuinely own.

One sign of a healthy coaching relationship, in my view, is this:

Over time, the client should stand more firmly on their own feet, not become more dependent on the coach.

05

The coach should be able to see the real work context

Executive coaching does not happen in a vacuum.

A leader works inside a real system. There is a CEO, a team, targets, organizational politics, limited resources and sometimes decisions with no clean answer.

One pattern I have seen repeatedly is that a question that first appears personal is not always only personal.

A client may say, 'I have lost confidence.' But as the conversation develops, it becomes clear that their role is ambiguous and two senior stakeholders are asking for contradictory things.

Or they may say, 'My team is underperforming.' Later it becomes clear that a conversation that needed to happen three months ago still has not happened.

A good coach should be able to see the person without losing sight of the system around them.

There is an important boundary, though.

Understanding the business context does not mean automatically becoming a consultant.

If a coach moves into another role because of their background or expertise, such as advisor, HR specialist or consultant, that shift should be made explicit.

Coaching and advisory work can both be useful.

The important thing is to know which work you are doing at any given moment.

06

Ask about confidentiality before you begin

This is not something I would leave to assumption.

Ask directly:

What remains confidential between me and the coach?

How are notes and session information stored?

If an organization is paying for the coaching, what information will be shared with a manager or HR?

Will the content of sessions be shared with an organizational sponsor?

In a professional coaching relationship, those questions should be answered before the work begins.

The current ICF Code of Ethics also emphasizes clear agreements about roles, responsibilities, confidentiality and how information is exchanged among the parties involved.

This becomes even more important in organizational coaching.

The organization may legitimately want to know why the engagement exists and what signs of progress should be visible.

That does not mean the organization is entitled to the private content of coaching sessions.

Those two things need to be separated.

07

Pay attention to how quickly the coach reaches an answer

This may sound counterintuitive, but I am cautious about coaches who find the answer very quickly.

Why?

Because the issue a client raises in the first five minutes is not always the real issue.

I have repeatedly seen a statement such as 'I lack confidence' turn, after some exploration, into 'I am afraid to disagree with my manager.'

Or 'I cannot manage my team' become 'I have been postponing a conversation I know I need to have for months.'

One principle that has become increasingly important in my own practice is:

Do not solve the first version of the problem too quickly.

Sometimes the most valuable thing a coaching conversation can do is not find a better answer, but discover what the real question is.

08

Know what kind of relationship you are entering

Coaching does not need to be completely predictable from the start.

But the relationship itself should not be vague.

Before you begin, you should have clarity about the initial purpose of the work, the likely rhythm of sessions, the coach's role, your own responsibility, confidentiality, fees and cancellation terms, and what happens if at some point you decide the relationship is no longer useful.

A large part of professional coaching is a clear agreement.

The ICF Code of Ethics requires clarity before coaching begins about the nature of the relationship, roles, responsibilities and the parties' right to end the relationship according to the agreement.

For me, that clarity is not just administration.

It is part of the quality of the coaching itself.

09

A few signs that make me cautious

A few things raise questions for me: guaranteed promotion, success or fixed outcomes; rapid diagnosis before the client has really been heard; unclear confidentiality boundaries; mixing coaching and consulting without making the change of role explicit; increasing client dependence on the coach; and excessive focus on the coach's models, tests or tools.

Tools can be useful. Models can be useful.

But the session should not become a showcase for the coach's toolkit.

The client is at the center of the conversation, not the coach's method.

010

So how do you choose the right executive coach?

I would not look for 'the best coach.'

I would look for the coach who is right for you and for the question you are facing now.

Check their recognized coaching credentials.

Look at their experience.

Ask about confidentiality.

Clarify the boundary between coaching and advisory work.

Most importantly, notice what happens to your thinking when you talk with them.

If you leave the conversation only impressed by the coach, that is not enough.

But if you can see your own question more clearly, notice something you could not see before, and still feel that the decision belongs to you, that is probably a better sign.

For me, the simplest summary is this:

Choose someone who helps you think better, not someone who thinks for you.

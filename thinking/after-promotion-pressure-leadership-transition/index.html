Why Does Pressure Increase After a Promotion?

Sometimes the new role moves faster than our sense of who we are in it

13 September 202618 min readPractice essay

Core question

Am I still playing my previous role at a higher level?

In brief

Sometimes the new role moves faster than our sense of who we are in it

Evidence note

The core of this article comes from recurring patterns observed in professional coaching experience: the shift from specialist to leader, difficulty with delegation, control versus trust, the psychological weight of responsibility, and the gap between a new title and an older professional identity. These are qualitative, aggregated observations from coaching practice, not a representative research sample of managers. They are used to form observations and questions, not statistical claims about all managers. Independent research is cited separately to test and contextualize these observations. Examples and patterns are written without identifying client detail.

01

Sometimes the new role moves faster than our sense of who we are in it

Sometimes a person is fully capable of a higher role, wants it, and has worked toward it for years—yet once they enter the role, they discover that what got them here is not enough for what comes next.

Across several executive coaching conversations with managers, one recurring pattern has been this: the person has entered management by title and responsibility, but part of how they still work belongs to the previous role. They still dive into the details themselves, solve problems personally, or delegate and then pull the work back toward themselves.

That experience has become a larger question for me:

“How many of us, after a promotion, are still playing our previous role at a higher level?”

02

The new role is not a larger version of the old one

Suppose you succeeded for years because you moved into problems quickly, saw the details, and found solutions.

That ability was probably one of the reasons you were given more responsibility.

But in the new role, that same strength—if it continues unchanged—can become a constraint.

You still enter every detail.

You delegate, then check the work again.

Wherever the team gets stuck, you step in to solve it.

And when the volume grows, the conclusion you reach is:

“I need to work harder.”

While the more important question may be:

“Am I still playing my previous role at a higher level?”

In reviewing recurring patterns across coaching conversations, this situation has appeared repeatedly: the person has entered management by title, but their professional identity is still largely built on being the specialist. Signs can include difficulty letting go, excessive entry into detail, or expecting work to be done in their own style.

Research also shows that moving into a leadership role is not merely learning a few new skills. A systematic review of 136 studies on entry into leadership roles identified three important processes in that transition: adjusting to the role, identity change, and the construction and social acceptance of a leadership identity in interaction with others.

A job title can change in a day.

Professional identity usually does not change that quickly.

03

Sometimes the issue is not workload—“everything rests on me”

In some sessions, the initial issue has been team management or too much work. But as the conversation continued, another pattern appeared: the person feels that if they do not step in, the work may not go right; if they are not careful, something will be missed; and if the result is poor, they are ultimately responsible.

This is not only a busy manager. There is a working logic behind it:

If I do not step in, the work may not go right.

If I am not careful myself, something will be missed.

If the result is poor, I am ultimately responsible.

In an aggregated review of more than a hundred coaching sessions, the same issue has appeared independently: as people grow, responsibility for the team, decisions, results and others becomes heavier. In some cases, the issue is not only the number of tasks; the psychological weight of responsibility is also part of the pressure.

Caution is needed here.

These observations do not mean that every manager who controls must be anxious, or that everyone who works a lot has an identity problem.

But the evidence does raise a serious question:

“Is this work truly part of my responsibility, or have I kept it because I still cannot imagine responsibility without control?”

04

What used to be a strength may later create a constraint

This theme has repeated across several other coaching paths as well.

Control can help someone be effective at one stage, but at the next stage the same pattern may block growth—and the new role may require trust and enabling others. In some cases, the person’s expertise drove past success, while the next stage needed leadership, influence and developing others.

That difference matters to me.

Sometimes there is nothing “wrong” in us that needs to be removed.

In fact, that same quality may have been an important part of our success.

The issue is that some strengths have a situational shelf life.

The ability to enter the details is excellent.

Until the details are no longer where you primarily need to be.

Responsibility is valuable.

Until it means no one else takes real responsibility.

Expertise is an advantage.

Until you feel you must know the answer to every question in order to prove your worth.

05

That is why a promotion is not always only a positive experience

We usually talk about promotion in the language of success:

More pay, more authority, a larger team, a higher position.

But the person’s experience can be more complex.

A longitudinal study compared 128 people who had been promoted with 150 similar people who had not been promoted over two years. The findings showed that the effect of promotion on job satisfaction, stress and some career-sustainability indicators is not uniform or simply positive.

The World Health Organization also lists under- or over-promotion among psychosocial risks at work, alongside high workload, role ambiguity, unsuitable hours and inadequate support.

So these two sentences can both be true at once:

“I wanted this promotion.”

And:

“This role is hard for me.”

Difficulty in the role does not necessarily mean the choice was wrong, or that the person is unsuited to it.

06

Part of the pressure comes from the need to prove oneself

In coaching sessions, promotion does not always appear only as a career goal.

In some conversations, promotion becomes linked with being seen, confirmation of capability, professional standing, or the question of whether the person truly belongs at the next level.

That is one of the important parts of the subject for me.

Because if the new role becomes a place to prove “I am good enough,” making a mistake is no longer only a work mistake.

Asking for help is no longer only asking for help.

Not knowing is no longer only not knowing.

Each can become a threat to the image of competence the person wants to protect.

Then the manager, instead of only asking:

“What decision does this situation need?”

May also be caught in another question at the same time:

“What does this decision show about me?”

Those two questions are not the same.

When promotion itself starts to feel like a test of self-worth, the companion essay on when promotion becomes a test of self-worth takes that question from another angle.

07

Sometimes what we call confidence is a different issue

In my sessions I have often encountered the subject of confidence.

But the further I have gone, the more cautious I have become about quickly diagnosing a “lack of confidence.”

Sometimes the person is capable, but does not count evidence of their own capability in their own favour. Sometimes they have not yet redefined success criteria for the new role.

For a newly promoted manager, a more useful question than “How do I increase my confidence?” may be:

“What is my criterion for knowing I am doing well in this role?”

If the criterion is still whatever produced success in the previous role, the answer will probably be misleading.

08

From “I do it myself” to “I get results through others”

This may be one of the hardest managerial shifts.

In the previous role, the quality of your own work was a large part of the result.

In a management role, you gradually need to build a result that largely happens without you doing the work directly.

This is not only a delegation skill.

It is a change in your relationship with control, mistakes, trust and even your own sense of worth.

For example, you have to tolerate that someone else will not do the work exactly in your style.

Or will make mistakes while learning.

Or will solve a problem with a solution that is not yours.

Here the question shifts from:

“How do I make sure everything is done right?”

To another question:

“How do I build a system that does not require everything to pass through me?”

Those are two entirely different models of management.

09

What can executive coaching do at this point?

The coach is not there to tell the manager:

“Delegate more.”

Or:

“Raise your confidence.”

Or to decide what work should go to whom.

For me, the value of coaching is more in creating a space where the manager can distinguish among several things:

The real pressure of the role, and the pressure they have added themselves.

The organization’s real expectation, and the expectation they imagine exists.

A skill issue, and an identity issue.

Responsibility that truly belongs to them, and responsibility they have kept out of habit.

Research by Kirschner and Terblanche with 686 managers from more than 300 organizations found that after coaching, managers spoke more about change in team leadership, communication, developing others and executive presence. The reported pattern from colleagues’ feedback was somewhat similar. Still, the study relies mainly on managers’ own reports and their retelling of others’ feedback, so it should not be read as proof of a definite causal relationship.

For me, coaching’s role sits here:

Not handing out a prescription, but helping the manager see what is hard to see in the middle of day-to-day pressure.

If the live question is whether coaching itself is the right support, the companion essay on how to choose an executive coach is a useful next read.

010

Perhaps “Am I ready for this role?” is not the most precise question

Because readiness for a higher role does not necessarily mean knowing every answer from day one.

Perhaps the real meaning of readiness is being able to recognize:

What is still useful,

What still needs to be learned,

And what once drove our success and now needs to change.

If after a promotion or taking on larger responsibility you feel pressure, before concluding “I am not suited to this role,” three questions may be worth pausing on:

“Which part of my previous role am I still carrying with me?”

“What work do I still think only I can do?”

And perhaps the hardest:

“If I did not need to prove something about my worth in this role, what would I do differently?”

Sometimes the next level does not only need a new skill.

It needs a new definition of ourselves.

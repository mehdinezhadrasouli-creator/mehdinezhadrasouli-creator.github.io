Coaching in Organizations

How Do We Know Organizational Coaching Actually Worked?

A good session is not the same as evidence of impact

14 September 202615 min readPractice essay

Core question

If an organization invests in coaching, what evidence shows that something has actually changed in real work?

In brief

A good session is not the same as evidence of impact

Evidence note

This article rests on two evidence layers. The first is my practical experience implementing an organizational coaching program whose architecture already included goals, observable behaviours, manager input, a midpoint review and a final review. That is experience working within the architecture, not a claim of proprietary authorship. The second is independent research on workplace coaching effectiveness and leaders’ reports of behavioural change after coaching. These sources do not allow the claim that coaching alone produced every business outcome. The narrower, defensible claim is that organizational coaching impact is better measured when the expected change is connected from the start to observable behaviour, an appropriate witness and a defined review point. No client, manager or organization is identifiable.

01

Impact has to show up outside the coaching room

The session ends and the client says:

“That was a really good session.”

For a coach, that is a welcome sentence to hear.

But when the organization is paying for coaching, another question remains:

What has actually changed in the workplace?

Did the manager make a different decision?

Have the conversation they kept postponing?

Delegate more?

Or did they simply have a session in which they thought well and felt better?

In organizational coaching, the quality of the conversation matters to me. But impact also has to be visible somewhere outside the coaching room.

02

“Better leadership” is still not a measurable goal

Many programs begin with aims such as:

“Strengthen leadership.”

“Build confidence.”

“Improve communication.”

These point in a useful direction, but they still do not say what is supposed to change.

If we do not define the change clearly at the start, almost any outcome at the end can be interpreted as success.

That is why, in organizational coaching programs, I prefer to translate one or two goals into observable signs.

Instead of “better delegation,” ask:

Which decisions should no longer stay only with the manager?

Or instead of “better conflict management,” ask:

In a real conflict, what different behaviour should become visible?

The point is not to turn everything into a number.

The point is to know from the beginning:

If coaching is useful, what change should show up in real work?

03

For me, measurement starts with three questions

In my experience implementing organizational coaching programs, three simple questions matter a great deal:

1. What is supposed to change? — Not a broad concept, but one or two specific behaviours or outcomes.

2. Who can see that change? — The leader themselves? Their manager? Team members? Or cross-functional stakeholders?

3. When will we look at it again? — If we only ask about impact at the end of the program, we lose an important chance to learn along the way.

That is why, in the current program architecture, beyond the opening conversation, a midpoint review and a final review are built in. At the midpoint, progress against the goals, the manager’s feedback on observable behaviours, and any change needed in the work environment are reviewed.

When the work environment itself is making change harder, that is often a system question worth examining before treating everything as an individual gap.

For me, this is not only reporting.

It is part of the intervention itself.

04

Measurement means seeing the path, not only the final score

Suppose a manager’s goal is to delegate more.

A few months later we may see that they have handed over some decisions — but every time the team makes a mistake, they take control back again.

That is not necessarily failure.

It is information.

In a midpoint review we can ask:

What has changed?

Where has the old behaviour returned?

What in the environment is making change hard?

Is the goal still the right one?

And what should the second half of the program focus on?

In this view, measurement is no longer only end-of-program work.

It becomes part of the learning.

05

Do not ask only the leader

The client’s own perspective matters.

Change may begin in awareness, judgment, confidence, or the ability to see more choices.

But leadership is not only an inner experience of the manager.

It happens in relationship with others.

Kirschner and Terblanche’s 2026 study examined responses from 686 executives across more than 300 organizations. Leaders most often spoke about change in areas such as team leadership, communication and people development, and the feedback they reported from others pointed in related directions.

The study does not prove that coaching alone created those changes; the data are largely self-reported. But it carries a practical message:

If the coaching goal is behavioural, it is better not to take evidence of change only from the individual.

06

Manager feedback is useful; session content is not

In organizational coaching, the manager above the client can be an important source for observing change.

They might say:

“They used to answer almost everything themselves; now they ask more questions.”

Or:

“They used to take issues back; now they let people carry their decisions through.”

That is useful data for assessing behaviour.

Knowing what was said inside the coaching session is something else entirely.

In the current program architecture, manager feedback covers only agreed, observable behaviours — not the content of private sessions.

Measuring impact should not erase confidentiality. Organizational coaching starts with the contract, not the first session goes deeper on that boundary.

07

Four things are better kept distinct

When we say coaching “had an impact,” we may be talking about four different levels:

Session experience — Was the coaching relationship useful and safe for the client?

Learning — Does the person see the issue differently, or find new choices?

Behaviour — Are they doing something different at work?

Work outcome — Has that behaviour affected a real result in their role?

The closer we move from individual behaviour to business result, the more other factors enter the equation.

That is why we should be careful about attributing an outcome to coaching.

08

On ROI, precision beats theatre

It is tempting to say:

“After coaching, team performance improved — so coaching produced that result.”

But in a real organization, usually more than one factor changes.

The market, structure, manager, team, goals or reward system may also have shifted.

Jones and colleagues’ meta-analysis of workplace coaching research reports positive overall effects on learning and performance. That does not mean every business outcome in a specific program can be attributed directly to coaching.

So my preference is to build an evidence chain:

Clear goal → observable behaviour → feedback → review over time → role-relevant outcome

If credible financial data also exist, they can be added.

But we do not need to invent artificial ROI for every human change.

09

Measure less, but more meaningfully

In an organizational coaching program, we usually do not need a long list of indicators.

For each manager:

One or two important goals

For each goal:

One or two observable signs

And across the program:

A starting point, a midpoint review and a final review

In the current program architecture, organizational competencies and the manager’s view help shape the goals, but the final goal is formed with the client’s participation. Progress is reviewed along the way and, when needed, the goal is reset by agreement.

For me, that is more useful than a long scoring form.

010

In the end, put three accounts side by side

In my view, a good evaluation of organizational coaching is not a single number.

It is better to look at three angles together:

What change does the client see in themselves?

What change do others see in their behaviour?

What has become different in real work?

If these three accounts are reasonably close, the evidence is stronger.

If they differ, that difference is valuable information too.

A manager may feel they are delegating more, while the team still does not see that change.

That gap itself can become an important focus for continued development.

011

Three questions before the next program

Before the next organizational coaching program begins, it can help to clarify only three questions:

Exactly which behaviour is supposed to change?

Who can see that change?

When will we look at it again so that measurement becomes part of learning?

If the answers to these three questions are not clear, the problem has probably not started with measurement.

It has started with a vague definition of success.

Questions to carry forward

  1. 01If we could choose only one behaviour to measure coaching impact, what would it be?
  2. 02Who can see a change in that behaviour in real work?
  3. 03When should we look at it again so that measurement becomes part of learning?

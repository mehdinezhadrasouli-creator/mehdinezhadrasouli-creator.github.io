Coaching in Organizations

Before Coaching the Leader, Check the System

Not every leadership problem is an individual problem

13 September 202612 min readPractice essay

Core question

Before choosing coaching as the intervention, what systemic factors should be examined alongside the leader’s behaviour?

In brief

Not every leadership problem is an individual problem

Evidence note

This article is grounded in professional experience across HR leadership, coaching, and the design of development interventions. These are qualitative professional observations, not a statistical study or a scientifically validated model. Independent research on context and work design supports the broader principle that behavior should also be understood in relation to situational constraints, autonomy, role demands, and the design of work. The limited claim of this article is that before choosing an individual intervention, examining systemic factors can improve the definition of the problem. No client, manager or organization is identifiable.

01

Is decisiveness really the problem?

Imagine an organization asks for coaching for one of its managers.

The initial diagnosis sounds clear:

“They need to be more decisive.”

At first, that seems reasonable. Perhaps the manager takes too long to make decisions, seeks too much reassurance, or avoids difficult disagreement.

But look more closely at the context and the picture may change.

Important decisions are repeatedly reopened higher up. Priorities shift. Information arrives late. The manager is accountable for results without having control over all the conditions that shape those results.

At that point, my question changes:

Is decisiveness really the problem?

Or is part of the behavior we want to change being produced or reinforced by the same system that now expects the manager to change?

That is one of the distinctions that matters to me in organizational coaching:

We work with the leader, but we do not separate the leader from the system around them.

02

A system problem can easily be translated into an individual trait

Organizations often describe development needs in familiar language:

“They need to be more strategic.”

“They need to delegate more.”

“They lack confidence.”

“They need to take more ownership.”

Any of those descriptions may be accurate.

But my experience across HR leadership and coaching has taught me to ask one more question before accepting the diagnosis:

What in this manager’s environment is reinforcing the very behavior we want to change?

In my professional work, the quality of a development intervention is often tied to factors such as clarity of the business problem, evidence, decision rights, organizational readiness, and ownership of execution. That has become a practical rule for me: before choosing an individual intervention, examine the system’s contribution too.

Organizational-behavior research points in the same direction. Gary Johns describes context as the situational opportunities and constraints that influence both the occurrence and meaning of organizational behavior.

Before we call something an individual gap, it is worth asking what role the system plays in creating it.

03

Two questions need to stay on the table

When a leadership-development request comes in, I prefer to hold two questions at the same time:

What does this leader need to see, choose, or do differently?

And:

What in the surrounding system makes that behavior easier or harder?

If we ask only the first question, we may transfer the organization’s responsibility onto the individual.

If we ask only the second, we may ignore the leader’s own agency and responsibility.

The point is not to find the “culprit.” It is to understand the contribution of the person and the contribution of the system.

04

Five questions before finalizing a coaching goal

Before I finalize a coaching goal in an organizational setting, I review five areas.

These are not a scientifically validated model. They are a practical diagnostic checklist informed by professional experience and evidence.

1. Decision rights — Is the leader accountable for an outcome they actually have enough authority to shape? If we ask for decisiveness while their decisions are repeatedly reversed, caution may not be only a personality issue.

2. Incentives and metrics — Does the organization reward the behavior it says it wants? We may tell a manager to delegate, but when something goes wrong ask, “Why didn’t you control it yourself?” The system then pulls the manager back toward control.

3. Information — Does the leader have access to the information required to make good decisions? What appears to be poor decision-making can sometimes be decision-making under incomplete information.

4. Role clarity — Are accountability, authority, and success criteria clear? If several stakeholders hold conflicting expectations, part of what looks like a behavioral weakness may come from role ambiguity.

5. Leadership reinforcement — What do senior leaders actually reinforce? If the message is “make decisions” but every important decision is reopened above the manager, the system’s real message differs from its stated message.

Research on work design also shows that features such as autonomy, role demands, and the design of work are part of the conditions that shape people’s experience and behavior at work.

05

What I learned from my own work

This perspective did not come only from research.

In one of my own organizational-change experiences, part of the friction came from moving faster than the level of sponsorship, trust, and decision-right clarity around the change.

The lesson I took from it was simple:

Even when the diagnosis and change design are sound, the pace of intervention has to match organizational readiness and clarity of authority.

I treat that as a professional learning from my own experience, not a universal law.

In other situations, I have also seen an apparent “people problem” look different once we examined the actual workflow. Instead of assuming the answer was simply more headcount or more individual development, looking at process and cross-functional bottlenecks changed the definition of the problem.

That is why, when an organization says, “this manager needs to change,” I still see one more diagnostic step.

06

Seeing the system does not remove the leader’s responsibility

There is an opposite mistake we can make here:

“The system is the problem, so the leader cannot do anything.”

That is another oversimplification.

A manager may have limited authority and still be able to negotiate clearer boundaries. The structure may be ambiguous and they may still be able to clarify expectations. The organization may be controlling and they may still be able to lead their own team differently.

And sometimes, after examining the context, the organizational conditions are sufficiently supportive and the issue really does sit more clearly with the leader’s judgment, skill, or behavior.

So the more useful question is not:

“Is the problem the person or the system?”

It is:

“What is each contributing to the problem?”

A companion essay on where you can still start when the system will not fully change takes the agency side of that question further.

07

When is coaching the right intervention?

If the issue primarily concerns how the leader thinks, chooses, communicates, or leads — and they have real room to behave differently — coaching can be a useful intervention.

But if the main constraint is structure, authority, information, or incentives, coaching should not be expected to substitute for fixing the system.

Sometimes the right answer is both.

The leader works on their part while the organization changes part of the context.

A manager may work on delegation while their manager agrees not to take delegated decisions back. A leader may work on decisiveness while the organization clarifies the boundaries of their decision authority.

That is when coaching becomes more than an isolated individual-development program. It becomes part of a meaningful organizational intervention.

08

Where does the organizational sponsor fit?

When the organization pays for coaching, a sponsor is also part of the engagement.

The sponsor has a legitimate interest in why the organization is investing and what observable change the work is intended to support. That is not the same as having access to the content of coaching sessions.

The current ICF Code of Ethics requires clear agreement before coaching begins on roles, responsibilities, confidentiality, and how information will be exchanged among the client, sponsor, and other involved parties.

Organizational coaching starts with the contract, not the first session covers multi-party contracting and confidentiality in more depth. The point relevant here is narrower:

If the sponsor’s brief is simply “fix this person,” the problem has probably not been defined well enough yet.

09

Before saying “this manager needs to change”

These questions are worth pausing on:

What observable behavior is actually creating the problem?

What business outcome is that behavior affecting?

How much authority does the leader have to behave differently?

How does the current system reinforce the existing behavior?

What is the organization willing to change or support alongside the leader?

And then:

Where, specifically, can coaching add value?

Coaching should not become a way for an organization to hand a design, authority, or leadership problem to one person and say: “work on yourself.”

Sometimes the leader needs to work on themselves. Sometimes the organization needs to work on the system. In many real situations, both are necessary.

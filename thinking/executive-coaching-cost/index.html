How Much Does Executive Coaching Cost? What Actually Shapes the Fee?

Executive coaching fees are difficult to compare by hourly rate alone. Scope, cadence, sponsorship, assessments and progress review all affect what an engagement includes.

26 September 2026Updated 28 September 20264 min readPractical guide

Core question

When comparing two executive coaching proposals, what are you actually paying for?

In brief

Executive coaching cost is not only the price of an hour. A useful comparison looks at the scope of work, number and cadence of sessions, contracting, support and how progress will be reviewed.

Evidence note

The core of this article comes from my experience designing and delivering individual and organization-sponsored coaching engagements, including contracting, goals, cadence and progress review. ICF data is used only to show the wide variation in reported coaching fees globally; it is not presented as a recommended rate for Iran or for my own services. No client-specific financial or contractual information is published.

01

A number without the engagement design is incomplete

When someone asks, “How much does executive coaching cost?”, the tempting answer is to give a number. In my experience designing and delivering coaching engagements, though, the price of one session says very little unless the scope of the work is clear.

Two engagements can both be called executive coaching while being materially different. One may be six confidential online sessions. Another may be organization-sponsored work with three-party contracting, observable development goals, midpoint review and sponsor involvement. Comparing only the hourly fee hides those differences.

That is why I prefer to understand the work before quoting the final fee.

02

What has actually changed scope in my practice

In the engagements I have designed, session count, cadence and length are the first scope variables. Six sessions over three months are a different commitment from one standalone conversation.

Sponsorship changes the work more substantially. In organization-funded coaching, I have needed to contract purpose, confidentiality, sponsor input, information boundaries and progress-review points beyond the coaching hour itself.

Context matters too. I do not price a job title; I look at what the situation requires from contracting, stakeholder alignment and the design of the engagement.

When manager input, competency expectations, assessments or multi-rater data are added, I make explicit what is included and what is separate rather than hiding those elements inside one number.

Between-session support is another scope choice. Some engagements need none; others need limited follow-up around commitments or logistics. I treat that as designed work, not invisible free work.

A practical way to compare proposals is to separate the engagement into three layers: the coaching conversations themselves, the design work around them, and any organizational interface. The first is visible on the calendar. The second includes contracting, goal definition and review design. The third exists only when a sponsor or another stakeholder is part of the engagement. Two proposals with the same number of sessions can therefore represent different amounts of work and different expectations.

Experience and professional credential also belong in the comparison. A credential does not prove fit, but it does provide evidence about training, standards and professional accountability.

03

A global average still does not define the right price

For context, the 2023 ICF Global Coaching Study reported an average fee of US$244 for a one-hour coaching session in 2022. The same report showed substantial regional variation, from US$114 in Latin America and the Caribbean to US$277 in Western Europe.

I would not use that figure as “the right price” for executive coaching, or as a direct benchmark for Iran or any other local market. The variation is the point: geography, client mix and the design of the engagement matter.

04

Ask these questions when comparing proposals

If you are comparing two executive coaching proposals, I would ask more than “What is the price per session?”

Ask how the goals will be defined. How many sessions are proposed and at what cadence? If an organization is paying, how will sponsorship and confidentiality be handled? How will progress be reviewed? What, if anything, is included between sessions? And if the situation changes, can the engagement be redesigned?

Those questions make the commercial comparison more meaningful because they clarify what you are actually buying, not just how many hours appear on a calendar.

05

In my practice, the fee follows the scope

In my own practice, the first step is a 30-minute discovery conversation to understand the situation and test fit. Booking that conversation does not commit someone to a coaching programme.

If continuing makes sense, the purpose, shape of the engagement, cadence, responsibilities and fee are set out in writing. I prefer to price a real scope after understanding the situation rather than sell a fixed package first and force the question into it.

So my answer to “How much does executive coaching cost?” is simple: a number without the engagement design is incomplete. A useful proposal should make both the fee and what that fee covers clear. If the question is live for you, the executive coaching page explains how the work is structured.

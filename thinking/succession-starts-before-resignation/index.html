Succession planning starts before resignation, not after

A counteroffer after resignation is often too late. Succession work begins when people can still see a future inside the organization—and when internal mobility is treated as a success, not a loss.

7 September 202610 min readPractice essay

Core question

When does succession work actually need to begin?

In brief

A counteroffer after resignation is often too late. Succession work begins when people can still see a future inside the organization—and when internal mobility is treated as a success, not a loss.

Evidence note

Based on professional HR experience. No employer, employee, manager or coaching case is identified. Research sources are Gallup, McKinsey & Company and SHRM; full references and links appear in the Research sources section.

01

The counteroffer was not the point

He had already resigned. A few days later, the organization offered him roughly 40 percent more pay to stay.

The number was not what mattered. He had spoken, more than once, about his future in the organization long before he left.

He had about twelve years of experience. He was not demanding a promotion or insisting that the next role had to be his. He asked that, when the position opened, he would be one of the people considered: interviewed, and told if he was not ready—and what he would need to develop.

The message he received was that the organization would look at external candidates first. He resigned shortly afterwards. Only then did the organization start thinking about how to keep him.

02

Resignation is rarely where the problem begins

Organizations often treat the departure of a capable person as a sudden event: a competitor made an offer, the pay market moved, someone changed their mind.

What appears on the day of resignation is often the end of a longer process.

Gallup, studying people who had left voluntarily, reports that 42 percent said their manager or organization could have done something to prevent the departure. Nearly half—45 percent—said that in the three months before they left, neither a manager nor another leader had proactively discussed their job satisfaction, performance or future with the organization.

The more important point is not the exit statistic itself. People do not always leave on the day they resign. Many pull away earlier, when they no longer feel part of the organization’s future.

03

Succession is more than a name beside a role

When we talk about succession planning, the first picture is often a short list: two or three names attached to a few key roles.

If that is all succession is, a large part of the problem is missed. Succession planning asks who the capable people are, what they still need for the next role, what experience they should be given, who is responsible for their development—and, most of all, whether they themselves can see what future might exist for them here.

SHRM stresses the same point. Clarity about a growth path does not have to mean a promised promotion. Organizations can be clear about readiness criteria, the capabilities required and possible routes, without guaranteeing a specific seat.

The person in this account wanted exactly that. Not a promise. Not a special privilege. A real chance to be seen, and to understand where he stood on the path.

04

The question may not be retention

After a resignation, the organization’s question is often how much more to pay so the person will stay. That question may already be late. The earlier question is whether this person has to leave the organization in order to grow.

McKinsey argues that internal mobility should be treated as success, not as the loss of a manager’s headcount. One of its recommendations is to encourage leaders for internal retention: keeping capable people inside the organization even when they leave the current team.

That distinction matters. A manager may want to keep a strong person. In practice, “keeping” can mean holding them in the same role: no new project, no wider visibility, no interview for another internal role, no serious development plan. The person who might have moved from one team to another inside the same organization then moves to another company.

05

Three questions worth asking before a resignation

Seen operationally, three questions are useful for managers and HR.

If the next role for this person opened tomorrow, would they know the criteria by which they would be assessed? If the answer is no, the development path is still too vague.

Is internal talent visible only when a direct manager nominates them? Or is there a way for people to enter talent review, internal mobility or internal selection processes?

If this person were gone in six months, what would be lost—one individual, or knowledge, relationships, client trust, technical capability, operating experience, and perhaps a potential successor? That third question is one of the most important in succession work, because the work is not only about who sits in whose chair. It is also about the risk of losing capability.

06

A pay rise can solve one problem, not every problem

In that situation, a substantial pay increase was offered after the resignation. For someone else, it might have been enough. Here, the original issue was not compensation. It was the sense that years of experience were still not enough to enter an internal growth process.

Pay can close a compensation gap. It does not necessarily change the message: I cannot see myself in this organization’s future.

Gallup also shows that while compensation is an important part of what preventable leavers say could have kept them, a much larger share of those responses concerns how people are managed, organizational issues and opportunities to advance.

07

Succession work starts before the vacancy

Organizations often become serious about succession planning once an important seat is already empty. By then, many of the consequential choices have already been made. A capable person may have left. Knowledge may have gone with them. A manager may only now see the dependency. Replacement then happens in a hurry.

Real succession work starts earlier: when we talk about people’s futures, make growth criteria visible, refuse to confine development to the performance review, and treat a manager as responsible for talent even when it grows beyond their own team.

And while no one has resigned yet, it is worth asking: who in this organization is still waiting for a clear conversation about their future?

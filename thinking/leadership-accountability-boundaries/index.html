When Accountability Turns Into Over-Responsibility

High accountability can become less useful when a leader treats every outcome as personally theirs to carry, including choices and constraints that sit elsewhere.

27 September 2026Updated 28 September 20265 min readPractice essay

Core question

When something goes wrong in my team or system, what part is actually mine to own?

In brief

Healthy accountability means seeing your contribution accurately. When a leader sees only two positions, fully responsible or not responsible at all, learning, boundaries and delegation become harder.

Evidence note

The core of this article comes from several independent coaching observations and approved internal evidence derivatives: absorbing full responsibility for a systemic problem, moving between the poles of everything is mine to solve and there is nothing else I can do, and difficulty separating personal contribution from the contribution of others or the surrounding system. These are qualitative observations, not prevalence evidence about managers. Zero or hundred is a practical coaching lens, not a validated psychological construct. No client, organization or reconstructable situation is published.

01

High responsibility is not always the same as accurate accountability

In executive coaching, I sometimes work with leaders whose sense of responsibility is genuinely strong. They do not walk away from problems, results matter to them, and when something fails they look first at their own contribution. Those qualities often serve them well.

But the same strength can change shape. The leader stops owning only their part and begins absorbing decisions made by others, process failures, stakeholder reactions and constraints that were never fully within their control.

Across several coaching cases, I have seen responsibility experienced less as a proportion and more as a binary: zero or one hundred.

In the evidence behind this article, that distortion appeared in more than one form. In one case, a leader absorbed a systemic problem as if it were a personal failure. In another, the difficulty was separating their own contribution from decisions made by other people and constraints built into the role. That is why I treat over-responsibility less as a personality label and more as an attribution question: what is mine, what is shared and what belongs to the system?

02

Zero-or-hundred accountability makes learning less precise

If the only options are this is entirely mine or this has nothing to do with me, there is little room for diagnosis. The leader either carries the whole weight or pushes the problem completely outside themselves.

Most leadership situations are mixed. I may have made a weak decision while working with incomplete information. I may have failed to set a boundary while another person also failed to meet an obligation. I may have designed a process while several other people controlled how it was used.

For me, mature accountability starts with that distinction: neither escaping my contribution nor taking possession of everyone else's.

A simple way to make that distinction more concrete is to sort the situation into three buckets: mine, shared and not mine. Mine includes decisions or omissions I can change. Shared includes outcomes that depend on mutual action or interdependent roles. Not mine includes decisions and constraints I may influence but do not control. The categories are simple, but they can turn vague guilt into a more precise review of responsibility.

03

Over-responsibility can turn the leader into the bottleneck

When a leader believes everything eventually comes back to them, a practical consequence often follows: more control. Decisions are rechecked, work is harder to hand over, and other people's mistakes trigger rapid intervention.

This is where accountability and delegation meet. If I still experience myself as the real owner of every detail, assigning a task does not necessarily mean I have transferred meaningful authority.

There is a practical cost to getting that attribution wrong. If every result is ultimately “mine”, I am more likely to step back into work that should stay with someone else. If nothing is mine, I lose the chance to learn. The useful middle is specific ownership: naming my decision, my omission or my boundary without claiming the whole system.

The goal is not to care less. It is to become more accurate about which weight belongs to the role and which weight has simply accumulated around it. That precision also gives the team more room to own its part without the manager disappearing or taking over.

04

Replace a fault question with a more precise review

When something goes wrong, three questions can create a more useful review.

First: Which decision, action or omission was genuinely within my control?

Second: What belonged to another person, the system or external conditions?

Third: Given the part that is actually mine, what will I do differently next time?

These questions do not reduce accountability. They make it usable. When I can isolate my contribution, I can change something. When I claim the entire outcome, I mostly create more weight.

If this pattern is showing up through control, delegation or blurred boundaries in your role, the companion article on why managers struggle to delegate and the executive coaching page are useful next steps.

Questions to carry forward

  1. 01In the last problem I took personally, what was actually within my control?
  2. 02Am I carrying responsibilities that belong elsewhere under the label of being accountable?
  3. 03If my share is neither zero nor one hundred, what is the more accurate proportion and what can I learn from it?

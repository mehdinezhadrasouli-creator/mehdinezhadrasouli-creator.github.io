Coaching in Organizations

What Role Should the Direct Manager Play in Organizational Coaching?

Support the change without entering the confidential coaching conversation

14 September 202614 min readPractice essay

Core question

How can a direct manager support behavioural change without entering the confidential content of coaching sessions?

In brief

Support the change without entering the confidential coaching conversation

Evidence note

This article draws on my practical experience implementing organizational coaching and on independent research about executive coaching, feedback and the work environment. The evidence does not support a simple claim that a particular level of manager involvement causes a particular coaching outcome. The narrower claim is that a direct manager can help transfer change from the coaching conversation into real work, provided there is a clear boundary between supporting observable behaviour and entering confidential session content. No client, manager or organization is identifiable.

01

Neither absent from the process nor inside the confidential conversation

The coaching sessions have started.

Every two weeks the leader meets the coach, and the organization has invested in the program.

After a few sessions, the direct manager may ask:

“So what am I supposed to do now?”

One answer is:

“Nothing — coaching is confidential.”

In my view, that answer is incomplete.

Another answer is to keep asking what happened in the session, what the coach said and what the person is supposed to do next.

That is not the right path either.

In organizational coaching, the direct manager should neither stand completely outside the process nor enter the content of the sessions.

Their role is to support the change — not to manage the coaching process.

02

Confidentiality does not mean removing the manager

In Organizational coaching starts with the contract, not the first session, I wrote about multi-party contracting, the organizational sponsor’s role and confidentiality boundaries.

ICF also emphasizes that before coaching begins, roles, responsibilities, confidentiality and the kind of information exchanged among the parties should be clear.

Sometimes that principle is taken to an extreme:

Because the session is confidential, the direct manager should have no role at all.

Yet the direct manager may be one of the most important people who sees behavioural change in real work, creates room to practice it and gives feedback on it.

The boundary, for me, is clear:

The manager can talk about behaviour and outcomes — not about the content of the coaching session.

03

Clarify what change matters

Suppose the organization says:

“We want this manager to work on their leadership.”

That is still not a clear goal.

The direct manager can help make the organization’s expectation more precise.

For example:

Does it expect fewer decisions to be escalated upward?

Should the manager give the team more authority?

Is the quality of peer conversations the issue?

Or do they need a clearer stance in hard decisions?

That input is valuable.

But the direct manager or organizational sponsor can clarify the organization’s expectations; the coaching goal is better finalised with the client’s own participation.

If the person feels no connection to the goal, we are probably not looking at a real development agenda — more like an assignment the organization has set for them.

04

Do not only expect change; make room for it

This section is, for me, one of the most important parts of organizational coaching.

Suppose we expect a manager to delegate more.

But their direct manager still steps into every important decision.

Or we expect more independent decisions, yet even small choices need several layers of approval.

Or we ask them to handle conflict more directly, but every disagreement is quickly escalated upward.

In those conditions, the person may reach useful insight in coaching, while the daily environment keeps reinforcing the old behaviour.

I examined a related angle in Before Coaching the Leader, Check the System.

Here the question is more specific:

If I expect this manager to behave differently, does their work environment actually allow that behaviour?

Recent research on executive coaching also suggests that the coaching process cannot be seen as fully separate from the work environment, available resources and managerial relationships around the person.

That does not mean the direct manager is responsible for the coaching outcome.

It does mean an organization cannot ask for new behaviour on one side and preserve the same conditions that produced the old behaviour on the other.

05

Give feedback; do not ask for a session report

Some leadership changes are hard for the person to assess alone.

They may feel they listen more.

Delegate more.

Or enter fewer details in meetings.

Others may see a different picture.

This is where the direct manager can be an important source of evidence.

Not with this question:

“What did you talk about in the coaching session?”

But with a concrete observation:

“In the last few meetings I noticed you asked your team members more questions before answering yourself.”

Or:

“When a project hits trouble, you still take control back very quickly.”

That kind of feedback is about observable behaviour.

Session content is something else.

Research by Smither and colleagues also found that managers who worked with a coach, compared with another group, more often sought ideas and feedback from their managers for improvement, and later showed greater improvement in multisource ratings — though the size of that difference was not large.

What matters to me is not that the direct manager enter the coaching process.

It is that a developmental conversation at work can help transfer learning from the session into real behaviour.

06

The direct manager should not become a second coach

Support is not the same as control.

If after every session we ask:

“What did the coach say?”

“What did you agree to do?”

“Did you talk about me?”

the coaching relationship gradually shifts from a safe place to think into a reporting channel.

And what gets damaged is trust.

The organizational sponsor has a legitimate right to ask about expected change and observable results; the content of coaching sessions belongs to the confidential coach–client relationship.

That boundary is better made clear at the start of the program, rather than renegotiated repeatedly along the way.

07

Coaching does not replace performance management

Another boundary matters too.

Sometimes a manager is dissatisfied with someone’s performance, but has not had the necessary conversation with them.

Then the organization decides:

“Let’s get them a coach.”

In that situation, coaching can become a way of postponing managerial responsibility.

If the manager needs to give clear feedback, the coach cannot do that in their place.

If role expectations are unclear, they should be clarified first.

If a performance decision is needed, it belongs in the performance-management process.

Coaching can help the person understand feedback better, see their choices and build new behaviour.

But it should not carry messages the organization is unwilling to say directly.

08

In practice, what should the direct manager do?

For me, the manager’s role makes sense at three points:

Before the start: clarify what change matters for the role and the organization.

During the program: create room to practice the new behaviour and give feedback on what they actually observe.

At review points and at the end: say what has changed in real work and what still needs development.

Without needing to know what happened inside the coaching session.

That is also where How Do We Know Organizational Coaching Actually Worked? connects: impact becomes more meaningful when we do not rely only on the client’s own account and also look at observable behaviour.

09

One simple boundary

If I had to summarise this article in one sentence:

The direct manager can support the change without entering the coaching room.

That boundary both protects the client’s trust and keeps the organization connected to the program it has invested in.

It also does not place all responsibility for change only on the coach and the client.

Because new behaviour has to last in the same environment where the person works every day.

010

Three questions for the direct manager or organizational sponsor

1. Exactly what observable change do I expect to see?

2. What in the work environment could make that change easier or harder?

3. How can I give feedback without entering the confidential content of the sessions?

If the answers to these three questions are clear, the manager is more likely to support the person’s development rather than interfere with the coaching.

Why Do Managers Struggle to Delegate? When Trust and Control Get Entangled

Delegation problems are not always skill problems. Managers can get caught in a loop of lower trust, tighter control and repeated follow-up that unintentionally reinforces team dependence.

27 September 2026Updated 28 September 20265 min readPractice essay

Core question

When I say I cannot delegate, what am I actually afraid will move outside my control?

In brief

Delegation is more than handing off work. If the outcome, decision boundary, review point and acceptable risk are unclear, managers tend to swing between micromanaging and stepping away too far. Start by making explicit what is actually being delegated and what responsibility still stays with the manager.

Evidence note

The core of this article comes from a longitudinal executive-coaching case and my professional experience in HR leadership, where repeated checking, reduced trust and underused managerial authority appeared together. One case does not establish that every delegation problem follows the same mechanism. The loop described here is therefore a practical diagnostic lens, not a validated scientific model. Independent research on trust, delegation and felt trust is used to test and constrain the argument. No client, manager or organization is identifiable.

01

Sometimes the manager delegates the task but not the control

In executive coaching, delegation often arrives as a technical question: “How do I delegate more?” In some situations, though, the problem is not a missing technique. The manager has handed over the work but still checks almost everything again.

After a few misses, trust drops. The manager follows up more. The team learns to wait for reminders, seek another approval or avoid moving too far without the manager. The manager then sees that dependence and concludes, “If I do not stay close, the work will not move.”

That cycle can begin for legitimate reasons. Quality, safety, customer impact or accountability may genuinely matter. The problem starts when additional control no longer only manages risk; it also consumes the manager’s attention and leaves little room for others to build judgement.

In the longitudinal coaching case behind this article, repeated checking, reduced trust and underused authority did not appear as separate problems. They reinforced one another across sessions. The useful move was not simply to “delegate more”; it was to examine where the manager was still holding the decision, where follow-up had become automatic and which authority could be made explicit rather than silently retained.

02

Good delegation starts with the decision boundary

The most useful question I have found is simple: What exactly is being delegated? A task? The method? A decision? Or ownership of an outcome?

When that boundary is vague, manager and employee can hold different pictures of authority. One person thinks they own execution while the other expects them to own the decision as well. The predictable result is repeated intervention from above or a sense of being abandoned below.

A study of 215 unit managers in professional-services firms found that managers delegated more authority when employees held more task-specific knowledge and when monitoring costs were lower. Delegation, in other words, is not a binary virtue. It depends on capability, task conditions and the risk that still needs to be managed.

One practical test is to ask what would happen if the employee made a different but still acceptable decision. If the manager would immediately override it, the delegated boundary may not be as wide as both sides think. Delegation becomes more credible when there is room for judgement, not only room to reproduce the manager’s preferred method.

03

Trust is not only a precondition for delegation; it can also be an outcome

Another set of studies found that people felt more trusted when leaders delegated tasks to them, and the effect was stronger when the delegated task was important.

That does not mean more delegation is always better. Responsibility without authority, information or support can feel less like trust and more like burden transfer.

Before delegating, I therefore want four things to be explicit: What result are we aiming for? Which decisions can the person make? When will we review progress? And what threshold should bring the issue back to the manager?

04

If you still recheck everything, try these three questions

First: What real risk am I protecting with this level of control? If the answer is unclear, some of the control may be habitual rather than necessary.

Second: Have I defined success clearly enough for someone to make a reasonable decision without having to read my mind?

Third: Have I designed the review point in advance, or do I only re-enter the work when my own anxiety rises?

I have also seen that a planned review point feels very different from continuous monitoring. When manager and employee know in advance what will be reviewed, when it will be reviewed and what requires escalation, follow-up becomes part of the agreement rather than a signal that trust has been withdrawn.

Coaching in this situation is not about forcing a manager to “let go.” It is about seeing the difference between necessary control and control that has started to limit both the manager and the team. Questions to carry forward

  1. 01What part of the work I delegated have I still kept mentally for myself?
  2. 02Does my team know exactly where it can decide and where it should escalate?
  3. 03The last time I rechecked someone’s work, was I managing a real risk or my own discomfort?

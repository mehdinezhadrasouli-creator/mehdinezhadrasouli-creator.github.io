Coaching in Organizations

Organizational coaching starts with the contract, not the first session

When an organization sponsors coaching, the challenge is not choosing between confidentiality and accountability. It is designing an agreement that protects the client's thinking while making the purpose and observable outcomes clear.

7 September 202611 min readPractice essay

Core question

How can an organization know what coaching is meant to change without turning the coach into a reporter?

In brief

When an organization sponsors coaching, the challenge is not choosing between confidentiality and accountability. It is designing an agreement that protects the client's thinking while making the purpose and observable outcomes clear.

Evidence note

Based on professional HR experience, design work for organizational coaching engagements, the ICF Code of Ethics and 2026 research on 686 executives across more than 300 organizations. No client, manager or organization is identifiable; the program design described is not presented as a completed outcome study.

01

The first question is not how many sessions

When I design an organizational coaching engagement, one question matters before the number of sessions, the assessment tool or even the coach selection: what should the organization be able to see without entering the content of the individual coaching conversations?

The question matters because organizational coaching introduces a legitimate third party. The client needs a confidential place to think honestly. The sponsor has a legitimate interest in why the engagement exists and what kind of change the organization is investing in. Good design has to hold both realities at the same time.

02

The choice is not reporting or no reporting

I often see two unhelpful extremes. In one, coaching becomes a black box: a leader attends sessions for months and the organization hears little beyond 'the sessions went well.' In the other, the coach is treated as an information channel: what is the leader talking about, what is wrong with them, what does the coach think of their readiness, and what should the organization do next?

Neither is the model I want to build. The organization can have clarity about purpose, expected behaviour and signs of progress without receiving the content of private coaching conversations. Progress can be discussed without turning the coach into a reporter.

03

Confidentiality should be designed before the work begins

Imagine that after the fourth session an HR leader asks the coach, 'So, how is it going?' If nobody has agreed what that question can legitimately mean, the problem did not begin in session four. It began in the design of the engagement.

The current ICF Code of Ethics requires a clear agreement before coaching begins about roles, responsibilities, confidentiality and other aspects of the engagement. It also requires agreement about what information is exchanged and how it is exchanged among the parties involved.

In one recent organizational coaching program I designed, individual sessions were defined as confidential, while progress against agreed goals, feedback on observable behaviours and support needed from the work environment were reserved for multi-party review conversations. The distinction is important: the engagement can be visible without the private conversation becoming visible.

04

'Improve leadership' is still too vague

Organizational coaching also becomes weak when the goal is too broad: improve executive presence, become a better manager, communicate better. These may describe a direction, but they are not yet useful enough for contracting.

My experience in organizations has pushed me toward a more concrete question before a coaching engagement begins: which two or three behaviours should become more visible in the leader's day-to-day work? Delegating decisions that were previously held too tightly. Having a difficult conversation that has repeatedly been postponed. Asking more questions before offering a solution. The point is not to make every human change numerical; it is to connect development to work that can actually be observed.

A 2026 study analysed 1,130 open-ended survey responses from 686 executives across more than 300 global organizations. Leaders most frequently reported perceived improvements in team leadership, communication, people development and executive presence; the colleague feedback they reported showed related patterns. The study is not causal proof of coaching impact, and the colleague feedback is reported through the leaders themselves. Its practical value is narrower: leadership development becomes more useful to discuss when it is connected to behaviours that can be noticed in real work.

05

Who should be able to see the change?

If the goal is better delegation, who can actually notice the difference? The leader, the manager above them, team members, or cross-functional colleagues? That question should be considered before the final review, not introduced at the end of the program.

A light baseline before the engagement, a midpoint review and a final review can help show whether a new behaviour has entered daily work. This is not an argument for turning coaching into performance appraisal. It is an argument for making the intended change visible enough that the client and sponsor know what they are trying to support.

06

The coach is not the organization's assessor

One boundary matters especially to me: an organizational coach should not gradually become the hidden assessor of the client. Managers can give feedback about observable behaviour. HR can describe role requirements. The organization can make its own talent and performance decisions. Those responsibilities should remain where they belong.

In the programs I design, the coach does not provide personality reports, mental-health diagnoses, rankings, or recommendations about hiring, promotion or termination as a by-product of confidential coaching. Coaching should not become a covert evaluation process.

07

Sometimes the problem is not only in the manager

An organization may sponsor coaching because a manager does not delegate. A closer look may show that the manager has little real authority, that every decision is rechecked above them, that the cost of error is unusually high, or that the performance system rewards detailed control.

If all responsibility for change is placed on the individual, coaching can become an attempt to adapt one person to a system that is helping to create the problem. Organizational coaching keeps another possibility open: part of the change may belong to the leader, and part may belong to the role, manager, relationships or system around them.

08

A contracting architecture I prefer

There is no single structure for every engagement, but I usually prefer a sequence with five distinct moments. A session zero prepares the client for what coaching is and is not. An opening multi-party conversation aligns the client, sponsor and coach on purpose, observable behaviours, confidentiality and review. Individual sessions then remain protected as the client's thinking space.

A midpoint review looks at agreed behaviours and whether the work environment needs to change; it is not a report on session content. A final review asks what changed, what remains, and how the client will continue the learning without the coach. In a recent six-month program design, this architecture included a separate coaching agreement for each participant and explicit boundaries around what could be shared.

09

The organization should not know everything — but it should not know nothing

If the organization knows everything, the client does not have a credible private space for honest thinking. If the organization knows nothing, coaching can drift away from the role, the business context and the reason the organization invested in it.

A good organizational coaching contract holds both: private space for the person to think, and enough clarity about purpose and observable change for the engagement to remain connected to real work. That is why, for me, organizational coaching does not begin with the first coaching session. It begins with the contract.

Questions to carry forward

  1. 01If a leader enters coaching, which two or three behaviours should become different in their real work?
  2. 02Does the client know from the beginning what information can be shared with the organization and what remains confidential?
  3. 03Have we placed all responsibility for change on the individual, or have we also examined what may need to change in the role, manager, relationships or system?

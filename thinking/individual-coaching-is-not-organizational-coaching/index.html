Coaching in Organizations

Organizational coaching starts with the contract, not the first session

When an organization sponsors coaching, the challenge is not choosing between confidentiality and accountability. It is designing an agreement that protects the client's thinking while making the purpose and observable outcomes clear.

7 September 2026Updated 24 September 20265 min readPractice essay

Core question

How can an organization know what coaching is meant to change without turning the coach into a reporter?

In brief

Organizational coaching needs a clear agreement before individual sessions begin: purpose, observable outcomes, roles, confidentiality and review points. The sponsor can know what change is being pursued without receiving session content.

Evidence note

Based on professional HR experience, practical experience implementing organizational coaching programs, the ICF Code of Ethics and 2026 research on 686 executives across more than 300 organizations. No client, manager or organization is identifiable; the program architecture described reflects implementation experience, not proprietary design ownership or a completed outcome study.

01

The first question is not how many sessions

When I work within an organizational coaching engagement, one question matters before the number of sessions, the assessment tool or even the coach selection: what should the organization be able to see without entering the content of the individual coaching conversations?

The question matters because organizational coaching introduces a legitimate third party. The client needs a confidential place to think honestly. The sponsor has a legitimate interest in why the engagement exists and what kind of change the organization is investing in. A useful agreement has to hold both realities at the same time.

02

Confidentiality should be designed before the work begins

Imagine that after the fourth session an HR leader asks the coach, 'So, how is it going?' If nobody has agreed what that question can legitimately mean, the problem did not begin in session four. It began in the design of the engagement.

The current ICF Code of Ethics requires a clear agreement before coaching begins about roles, responsibilities, confidentiality and other aspects of the engagement. It also requires agreement about what information is exchanged and how it is exchanged among the parties involved.

In one recent organizational coaching program I worked within, individual sessions were defined as confidential, while progress against agreed goals, feedback on observable behaviours and support needed from the work environment were reserved for multi-party review conversations. The distinction is important: the engagement can be visible without the private conversation becoming visible.

03

'Improve leadership' is still too vague

Organizational coaching also becomes weak when the goal is too broad: improve executive presence, become a better manager, communicate better. These may describe a direction, but they are not yet useful enough for contracting.

My experience in organizations has pushed me toward a more concrete question before a coaching engagement begins: what are one or two important goals for this work, and what one or two observable signs would show progress on each? A goal around stronger delegation might become visible when decisions that were previously held too tightly begin to move to the right person. A goal around clearer communication might show up when a difficult conversation that has been postponed finally happens, or when more questions come before solutions. The point is not to make every human change numerical; it is to connect development to work that can actually be observed.

A 2026 study analysed 1,130 open-ended survey responses from 686 executives across more than 300 global organizations. Leaders most frequently reported perceived improvements in team leadership, communication, people development and executive presence; the colleague feedback they reported showed related patterns. The study is not causal proof of coaching impact, and the colleague feedback is reported through the leaders themselves. Its practical value is narrower: leadership development becomes more useful to discuss when it is connected to behaviours that can be noticed in real work.

04

The organization should not know everything — but it should not know nothing

If the organization knows everything, the client does not have a credible private space for honest thinking. If the organization knows nothing, coaching can drift away from the role, the business context and the reason the organization invested in it.

A good organizational coaching contract holds both: private space for the person to think, and enough clarity about purpose and observable change for the engagement to remain connected to real work. That is why, for me, organizational coaching does not begin with the first coaching session. It begins with the contract.

Questions to carry forward

  1. 01If a leader enters coaching, what are one or two important goals for the work — and what one or two observable signs would show progress on each?
  2. 02Does the client know from the beginning what information can be shared with the organization and what remains confidential?
  3. 03Have we placed all responsibility for change on the individual, or have we also examined what may need to change in the role, manager, relationships or system?

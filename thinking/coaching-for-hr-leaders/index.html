Coaching for HR Leaders: When HR Needs a Place to Think, Too

HR leaders do not always need another HR answer. Sometimes the live issue is their own decision, role boundary, influence or professional direction, and coaching creates a different kind of thinking space.

27 September 2026Updated 28 September 20265 min readPractice essay

Core question

When HR itself is stuck in a difficult decision, does the leader need expert advice or a place to think?

In brief

For HR leaders, an important distinction is knowing when they need HR expertise and when they need a space where they remain the owner of the question and the decision. Coaching does not replace domain expertise; it does different work.

Evidence note

The core of this article comes from three forms of primary evidence: my own HR leadership experience, my experience receiving coaching during a professional transition, and a coaching engagement with an HR professional where the boundary between a request for HR advice and the agreed coaching goal became explicit. These are limited qualitative sources and are not used to make prevalence claims about all HR leaders. ICF standards are used only to clarify role boundaries and client ownership. No client, organization or reconstructable situation is published.

01

HR is not always supposed to know the answer

HR leaders often occupy the place other people come to for a framework, an answer or a decision. But HR leaders can face questions where technical HR knowledge is not enough: whether to stay or leave, whether to take a role, how to set a boundary with a CEO, how to influence a system they do not fully control, or what the next professional chapter should be.

I have experienced this from both sides: as an HR leader who has used coaching during a transition, and as a coach working with an HR professional. That two-sided experience has made one distinction clearer for me: knowing HR is not the same as thinking clearly about your own situation.

During one transition of my own, the questions that mattered most were not technical HR questions. I already knew how to evaluate roles, structures and people decisions. What I needed was room to notice what I wanted, what I was avoiding and which trade-offs I was actually willing to make. That experience is one reason I do not assume expertise is the answer just because the client and I share the same profession.

02

Sometimes a request for HR advice is sitting on top of another question

In one coaching engagement, an HR professional asked whether we could also use my HR expertise. It was a reasonable request. But the goal we had contracted for was different: greater clarity about professional direction, stronger self-understanding and a more deliberate next career decision.

The important part was not to blur the roles. If I am giving advice, we should name it as advice. If I am coaching, the client should remain the owner of the issue and the choice. Sometimes the best help for an HR leader is a direct technical answer. At other times, a ready-made answer is exactly what shortens the thinking too soon.

In that HR-professional engagement, keeping the boundary explicit protected the original goal. We could acknowledge where my domain experience might be useful without turning every difficult choice into a request for my recommendation. The distinction sounds small, but it changes who is doing the thinking and who remains accountable for the decision.

03

For an HR leader, confidentiality can be part of the value

In my HR leadership experience, difficult people decisions are often entangled with several relationships at once: the CEO, the HR team, senior leaders, employees and sometimes a board or shareholder. Not every question can be explored freely with every person inside that system.

Coaching can offer an external, confidential space to examine assumptions, fears, authority boundaries and real options. That does not require the coach to know HR better than the client. The value can be that the HR leader is temporarily released from the role of the person who is expected to have the answer.

There is also a useful discipline when the same practitioner can both coach and advise. If domain input would genuinely help, I prefer to make the shift explicit: pause, agree that we are moving into advice or mentoring for a moment, then return to coaching. That small reset protects the thinking space and prevents shared HR experience from quietly becoming the default answer.

04

Before choosing a coach or adviser, ask three questions

First: Is my problem a gap in expertise, or is it the quality of my thinking around a decision? If it is primarily an expertise gap, consulting or mentoring may be more direct.

Second: Do I need someone to transfer experience to me, or do I need to make the trade-offs, meaning and choice clearer for myself?

Third: Can I examine this issue freely inside the organization? If not, the confidentiality and independence of the coaching relationship may matter more.

The 2025 ICF Core Competencies keep responsibility for choices with the client and require coaches to maintain distinctions between coaching, consulting and other support professions. That boundary matters for HR leaders, too. If the live issue is a decision, role or influence question, executive coaching is the relevant path. If you need analysis and explicit HR recommendations, strategic HR advisory uses a different contract.
